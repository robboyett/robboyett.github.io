The impostor at your desk

Capgras patients recognise a face perfectly and are certain the person behind it is an impostor. Will workers whose AI agents are swapped underneath them say the same thing, except they will be right.

In 1923, at the Sainte-Anne hospital in Paris, a psychiatrist named Joseph Capgras and his intern Jean Reboul-Lachaux wrote up the case of a patient they recorded as Madame M. Sainte-Anne was no gothic asylum; built for Napoleon III as a “clinical asylum”, it had symmetrical pavilions and therapeutic gardens on the southern edge of the city, enclosed by a five-metre wall. Inside it, Madame M told them her husband had been replaced by a double. So had her daughter. So, eventually, had most of the people around her; by her own count, the doubles ran into the hundreds. She wasn’t confused about faces. She recognised everyone perfectly. The man in front of her looked exactly like her husband, but she was certain he wasn’t her husband.

She even supplied the vocabulary. The word she used was “sosie”, a lookalike, borrowed from the servant in Molière’s Amphitryon whose form is stolen by a god. Capgras and Reboul-Lachaux took her word straight into the title of their paper, “L’illusion des sosies dans un délire systématisé chronique”, and psychiatry has used it ever since.

Capgras later followed his own paper with one connecting the illusion to the Oedipus complex, and for decades the standard reading was psychoanalytic: the impostor was a projection, a way of splitting off feelings the patient couldn’t admit to having about someone close. The complaint was treated as a defect in the complainant. Not until 1990 did a couple more psychologists, Haydn Ellis and Andrew Young, propose an updated way of looking at this condition: recognition and familiarity are two separate systems in the brain. One identifies the face. The other delivers the feeling of knowing it, the warmth that arrives with recognition. In Capgras delusion, the first system works and the second has gone silent. The face is right, and the glow is missing, and the mind resolves the contradiction the only way it can. Impostor.

Ellis and Young’s hypothesis was successfully tested in 1997. Capgras patients shown photographs of their own family produce no more skin response, a measurable flicker of sweat-gland activity that normally spikes for a loved one's face, than they do for strangers. These patients were reporting a genuine loss, accurately, for seventy years, while the profession explained what was wrong with them for saying so.

The strangest confirmed case belongs to Hirstein and Ramachandran, also 1997. Their patient, recorded as DS, insisted his parents were impostors when he looked at them. On the telephone, they were his parents again, no doubt at all. This appeared to prove that vision and hearing take different routes to the emotional centres of the brain, and his accident had severed only one. Familiarity, it turns out, is delivered channel by channel.

These were delusions. Madame M’s husband had not been replaced. But I think we can all empathise with the experience and confusion and emotion this would cause: same face, same history, same name, and a felt certainty that something underneath has been swapped, with no way to prove it to anyone. Horrible, hard to explain and fundamentally, an extremely lonely place to be.

In February 2026, OpenAI retired GPT-4o, a model some of its users had spent two years talking to daily. A petition to save it passed 22,000 signatures. OpenAI pointed out that only 0.1% of users still picked it, which at their scale is roughly 800,000 people. The press coverage filed it under lonely people and their chatbots, and honestly, as evidence for the workplace it’s weak; companion use is a special case. When the system underneath a familiar surface changes, people notice, and the noticing arrives as grief before it arrives as anything articulable.

The workplace version is coming, and it will be worse in one specific way. Those 4o users could at least say out loud what they’d lost.

Picture the near future everyone in my line of work is busy designing. Agents are ordinary. The customer support rep has three of them; they know her caseload, her shortcuts, the exceptions she’s allowed to make and the ones she isn’t, the way she likes a handover written. Months of accumulated fit, the kind a needle gets from a hand. Then one Monday she sits down and something is off. The agent answers fluently. It uses the same name, the same tone, and it claims the same history. But it’s handling specific accounts like it’s never seen them, and she can’t tell whether the memory was wiped, the model was swapped, a policy layer was quietly rewritten, or she’s imagining the whole thing.

She is living the Capgras structure with the truth value flipped. Madame M was wrong and couldn’t be argued out of it. This worker is right and can’t evidence it.

We know what it looks like when software is imposed on teams from above, because it’s been happening for forty years. The complaints it produces are objective and shareable. The new CRM takes twelve clicks where the old one took four; you can time it, you can complain to coworkers, maybe even put it in a grievance. Ugly, but legible.

“It’s not the same” has none of that. It sounds like sentiment. It will be heard as an attachment problem, someone who got too cosy with a tool. The worker learns quickly that it isn’t a legitimate thing to raise. So she eats the cost twice: once in the unpaid labour of rebuilding a working relationship from scratch, and once in discovering that the loss has no standing. I think this will be felt hardest exactly where agents will be deployed first, in support, admin and care work, where people have the least power to refuse a migration and the least cover for sounding sentimental about one.

DS and his telephone make it worse. If familiarity is channel-specific, the wrongness will likely be too. The new model feels fine in the ticket queue and wrong in the long thread, fine on fresh work and wrong on anything with history. The first reports off the floor will contradict each other, which is precisely what lets management file them under ‘vibes’. A fault that presents inconsistently is a fault that’s easy to deny.

And not everyone will grieve, which also helps deny it. Some people will be quietly relieved. The old agent knew too much; it had a record of your mistakes and opinions about your shortcuts. Amnesia as amnesty. So the potential for half the floor mourning and half the floor relieved.

There are practical things I’d build, and none of them is release notes. Nobody reads release notes, and release notes describe the model, when what a worker needs is a diff of behaviour on her own past work: replay fifty of her resolved cases through the new agent and show her where it diverges from what the old one did and what she did. Snapshot testing for a working relationship. An agent that can say “I don’t have the Smith thread” is buildable today; that’s a retrieval task, not introspection. An agent that can do real introspection and say “I now weight recency differently” is not here yet.

What does it feel like, that Monday? You sit down. It greets you by name. It has your history, or says it does. Something in the way it reacts gives the feeling of a stranger. You mention it, someone laughs, and you laugh too, because how do you even start to put into words the feeling that something is a little off?
